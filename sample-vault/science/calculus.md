# Calculus

Calculus is the mathematics of continuous change, built on the twin ideas of the derivative, which measures instantaneous rates, and the integral, which accumulates quantities.

It was developed independently in the late seventeenth century by [[people/isaac-newton|Isaac Newton]] and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, whose priority dispute divided European mathematics for decades; Leibniz's notation, including the dy/dx and integral signs, is the one still used today. Newton invented his version, which he called the method of fluxions, largely to solve the problems of [[science/newtonian-mechanics]], and the pairing of calculus with experiment became a template for the [[science/scientific-method]] across physics.

## Why it mattered

Calculus turned qualitative questions into solvable equations. Planetary orbits, the flow of heat, Maxwell's field equations in [[science/electromagnetism]], and the [[space/rocket-equation]] that governs spaceflight are all differential equations, and the machinery for handling them descends directly from Newton and Leibniz. The fundamental theorem of calculus, linking derivatives and integrals as inverse operations, remains one of the most consequential results in mathematics.
