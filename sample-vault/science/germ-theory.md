# Germ Theory

Germ theory holds that many diseases are caused by microorganisms too small to see, which invade the body and multiply. Replacing older ideas about "bad air," or miasma, it became a foundation of modern medicine and public health.

In the nineteenth century Louis Pasteur showed that microbes cause fermentation and spoilage, and Robert Koch established rigorous criteria linking specific bacteria to specific diseases. Their careful experiments exemplified the [[science/scientific-method|scientific method]], using controlled conditions, sterilization, and reproducible results to overturn the doctrine of spontaneous generation. The work justified antiseptic surgery, vaccination, and sanitation.

Germ theory later connected to genetics and heredity: understanding how pathogens mutate and adapt draws directly on the [[science/theory-of-evolution|theory of evolution]], while molecular tools built on the [[science/dna-double-helix|DNA double helix]] now identify organisms by their genomes. Its insistence on observable evidence also reflects the spirit of [[philosophy/empiricism|empiricism]].

## Why It Mattered

- Explained contagion and the spread of epidemics
- Justified handwashing, sterile instruments, and clean water
- Opened the path to antibiotics and vaccines
