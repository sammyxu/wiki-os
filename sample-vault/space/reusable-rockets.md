# Reusable Rockets

Reusable rockets are launch vehicles whose major components — typically the first-stage booster — return to Earth, land intact, and fly again. By recovering hardware instead of discarding it in the ocean, reusability has cut the cost of reaching orbit dramatically and reshaped the economics of [[space/space-exploration|space exploration]].

The idea is old — the Space Shuttle (1981–2011) reused its orbiter and boosters, though refurbishment proved costly — but the modern breakthrough came on December 21, 2015, when SpaceX landed a Falcon 9 first stage vertically at Cape Canaveral. Blue Origin's suborbital New Shepard had landed a booster a month earlier. Individual Falcon 9 boosters have since flown more than twenty missions each, ferrying crews and cargo to the [[space/international-space-station|International Space Station]]. The physics remains unforgiving: the [[space/rocket-equation|rocket equation]] means every kilogram of landing legs and reserve propellant trades against payload, so a booster must be cheap to refly for the math to pay off.

## Why it matters

- Booster recovery can cut launch prices several-fold compared with expendable vehicles
- High flight rates enable megaconstellations and frequent science missions
- Contrast with the [[space/apollo-program|Apollo Program]]'s Saturn V, discarded entirely each flight
- Propulsive landing is applied [[science/newtonian-mechanics|Newtonian mechanics]]: engines relight to null velocity precisely at touchdown
