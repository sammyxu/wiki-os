# Marie Curie

Marie Curie (1867-1934) was a Polish-French physicist and chemist who pioneered the study of [[science/radioactivity]] and remains the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences, Physics in 1903 and Chemistry in 1911.

Working in Paris with her husband Pierre, Curie showed that the mysterious rays emitted by uranium were an atomic property of matter, coining the term "radioactivity." In 1898 the Curies announced two new elements, polonium and radium, adding fresh entries to the [[science/periodic-table|periodic table]] after processing tonnes of pitchblende by hand.

## Life

Born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867, she moved to Paris in 1891 to study at the Sorbonne, where she later became its first female professor. After Pierre's death in 1906 she continued their research alone, and during the First World War she equipped mobile X-ray units, the "petites Curies," to treat wounded soldiers at the front.

## Legacy

Curie's work opened the door to nuclear physics and, alongside the emerging [[science/quantum-mechanics|quantum mechanics]] of her contemporaries such as [[people/albert-einstein|Albert Einstein]], reshaped the picture of the atom. She died in 1934 of aplastic anemia caused by long radiation exposure, and her notebooks remain radioactive to this day.
