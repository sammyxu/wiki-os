# Richard Feynman

Richard Feynman (1918-1988) was an American theoretical physicist who shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics for developing quantum electrodynamics, the theory that joins [[science/quantum-mechanics|quantum mechanics]] with [[science/electromagnetism]].

Feynman's path-integral formulation and his famous diagrams gave physicists a practical way to compute how light and matter interact, reconciling quantum theory with the demands of [[science/special-relativity|special relativity]]. His *Lectures on Physics*, delivered at Caltech in the early 1960s, remain among the most admired introductions to the subject.

## Life

Born in Queens, New York in 1918, Feynman studied at MIT and Princeton before joining the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, where he led a computation group and worked alongside [[people/john-von-neumann|John von Neumann]]. After the war he taught at Cornell and then Caltech, later contributing early ideas on nanotechnology and quantum computing. In 1986 he memorably demonstrated the cause of the Challenger disaster by dropping an O-ring into ice water at a televised hearing.

## Legacy

Beyond QED, Feynman shaped how science is taught and popularized, insisting that genuine understanding beats memorized formulas. His curiosity-driven style, captured in *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*, continues to draw students into physics.
