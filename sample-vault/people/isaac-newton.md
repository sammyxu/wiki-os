# Isaac Newton

Isaac Newton (1642-1727) was an English mathematician and physicist whose laws of motion and universal gravitation, published in the "Principia" of 1687, established [[science/newtonian-mechanics|Newtonian mechanics]] as the framework of physical science for over two centuries.

During the plague years of 1665-1666, working alone at Woolsthorpe, Newton developed the method of fluxions — his form of [[science/calculus|calculus]] — decomposed white light with prisms, and began thinking about gravity. A later priority dispute with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz over the invention of calculus embittered his final decades.

## Life

Born at Woolsthorpe Manor in Lincolnshire, Newton studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he became Lucasian Professor of Mathematics in 1669. He built the first practical reflecting telescope, published "Opticks" in 1704, served as Master of the Royal Mint, and presided over the Royal Society from 1703 until his death. His insistence on deducing laws from observed phenomena helped codify the [[science/scientific-method|scientific method]].

## Legacy

Newton showed that heavens and Earth obey the same laws derived from [[philosophy/first-principles|first principles]], a unification unmatched until [[people/albert-einstein|Albert Einstein]] recast gravity in general relativity. "If I have seen further," he wrote, "it is by standing on the shoulders of giants."
