# Rosalind Franklin

Rosalind Franklin (1920-1958) was a British chemist whose X-ray crystallography produced the crucial evidence for the [[science/dna-double-helix|DNA double helix]], one of the defining discoveries of twentieth-century science.

At King's College London in the early 1950s, Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling captured Photograph 51, an X-ray diffraction image whose sharp cross pattern pointed unmistakably to a helical structure. Shown to James Watson without her knowledge, it informed the 1953 Watson-Crick model of DNA, a triumph of the [[science/scientific-method]] in which her rigorous measurements were the decisive data.

## Life

Born in London in 1920, Franklin studied physical chemistry at Cambridge and earned her PhD in 1945 for work on the porosity of coal. After mastering X-ray diffraction in Paris, she moved to King's College and later to Birkbeck College, where she led pioneering studies of the tobacco mosaic virus and polio virus before her death from ovarian cancer at 37.

## Legacy

Franklin died four years before the 1962 Nobel Prize recognized the double helix, and her contribution went underacknowledged for decades. Like [[people/marie-curie|Marie Curie]] before her, she is now celebrated as a trailblazing woman in science, and DNA's structure explains the heredity at the heart of the [[science/theory-of-evolution|theory of evolution]].
