# Self-Organization

Self-organization is the process by which global order arises from local interactions among the parts of a system, without any central controller or external blueprint. Flocking birds, termite mounds, market prices, and convection cells all organize themselves.

The concept was sharpened by cybernetician W. Ross Ashby in the 1940s and given thermodynamic footing by Ilya Prigogine, whose work on dissipative structures — ordered patterns sustained by a flow of energy, like Bénard convection cells — earned the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Craig Reynolds's 1986 "boids" simulation showed that three simple steering rules reproduce lifelike flocking, a vivid demonstration that coordination needs no coordinator.

Self-organization is the mechanism behind [[systems/emergence|emergence]]: local rules plus [[systems/feedback-loops|feedback loops]] yield structure at the level of the whole. It is a central theme of [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]] and [[systems/complexity-theory|complexity theory]], with roots in [[systems/cybernetics|cybernetics]] and close ties to the nonlinear dynamics of [[systems/chaos-theory|chaos theory]] — order and chaos are two outcomes of the same underlying processes.

## Conditions that favor it

- Many interacting components following simple local rules
- Positive feedback to amplify useful patterns, negative feedback to stabilize them
- A steady flow of energy or information through the system
- No requirement for central control or global knowledge
