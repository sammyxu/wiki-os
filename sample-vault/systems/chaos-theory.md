# Chaos Theory

Chaos theory studies deterministic systems whose behavior is nevertheless unpredictable, because tiny differences in starting conditions grow exponentially over time. It showed that simple rules can generate behavior that looks random — and that long-range prediction has fundamental limits.

Henri Poincaré glimpsed the phenomenon in the 1890s while studying the three-body problem, but the field crystallized in 1963 when meteorologist Edward Lorenz found that rounding a number in a weather simulation produced a completely different forecast. His "butterfly effect" and the Lorenz attractor became emblems of sensitive dependence on initial conditions. In 1976 Robert May showed the same chaos hiding in the one-line logistic map of population biology, and Mitchell Feigenbaum discovered universal constants governing the route from order to chaos.

Chaos was a humbling amendment to the clockwork confidence of [[science/newtonian-mechanics|Newtonian mechanics]]: determinism does not imply predictability. For [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]], it explains why nonlinear [[systems/feedback-loops|feedback loops]] defeat naive forecasting, and it laid groundwork for [[systems/complexity-theory|complexity theory]] and the study of [[systems/emergence|emergence]].

## Hallmarks of chaos

- Sensitive dependence on initial conditions (the butterfly effect)
- Deterministic rules producing apparently random trajectories
- Strange attractors with fractal structure
- Universal period-doubling routes into chaotic behavior
