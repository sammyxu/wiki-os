# Pareto Principle

The Pareto Principle, often called the 80/20 rule, holds that in many systems roughly 80 percent of effects flow from about 20 percent of causes. It is one of the most frequently cited patterns in [[systems/systems-thinking]] because it reminds analysts that inputs are rarely equal in influence.

The idea traces to the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, who observed in 1896 that about 80 percent of Italy's land was owned by 20 percent of its population. In the 1940s the quality-management pioneer Joseph Juran generalized the pattern, coining the phrase "the vital few and the trivial many." The skewed distributions the principle describes are power laws, which recur throughout [[systems/complexity-theory]] — in city sizes, word frequencies, and wealth. Reinforcing [[systems/feedback-loops]], such as the rich-get-richer dynamics behind [[systems/network-effects]], are a common mechanism that produces them.

## Applications and cautions

- Software: fixing the most-reported 20 percent of bugs often eliminates the majority of crashes.
- Business: a small fraction of customers typically generates most revenue.
- Learning: [[writing/deliberate-practice]] concentrates effort on the few weaknesses that yield the largest gains.
- Caution: the exact 80/20 split is illustrative, not a law of nature; many real distributions are skewed but not precisely in that ratio.
