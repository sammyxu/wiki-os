# Information Theory

Information theory is the mathematical study of how information can be quantified, compressed, and transmitted reliably over noisy channels. It gave engineering a precise unit of information — the bit — and set hard limits on what any communication system can achieve.

The field was founded almost single-handedly by [[people/claude-shannon|Claude Shannon]] in his 1948 Bell Labs paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication." Shannon defined entropy as a measure of uncertainty, proved that every channel has a maximum capacity, and showed that error-free communication is possible below that limit using clever coding. His framework deliberately ignored meaning, which is exactly what made it universal: the same mathematics governs telephone lines, DNA, and deep-space probes.

Shannon's ideas connect naturally to the abstract model of computation in the [[computing/turing-machine|Turing machine]], and his notion of entropy mirrors the statistical entropy of [[science/thermodynamics|thermodynamics]] — a parallel Shannon himself noted. Alongside [[people/norbert-wiener|Norbert Wiener]]'s work on [[systems/cybernetics|cybernetics]], information theory became a core lens for [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]], treating organization itself as something measurable.

## Key ideas

- Entropy: the average uncertainty (in bits) of a message source
- Channel capacity: the ceiling on reliable transmission rate
- Redundancy and error-correcting codes as a defense against noise
- Data compression as the removal of statistical redundancy
