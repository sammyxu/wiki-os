# Network Effects

A network effect exists when a product or system becomes more valuable as more people use it. Each new participant benefits every existing one, so growth feeds on itself — a defining dynamic of the modern internet economy.

The idea predates computing: AT&T president Theodore Vail argued in 1908 that the value of a telephone lay in the network it reached. Robert Metcalfe, co-inventor of Ethernet, later popularized the rule of thumb that a network's value grows roughly with the square of its users. The [[computing/arpanet|ARPANET]] and then the [[computing/world-wide-web|World Wide Web]] — built by [[people/tim-berners-lee|Tim Berners-Lee]] on open protocols — became the largest demonstrations in history, as every new site and user made the whole web more useful.

In the vocabulary of [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]], network effects are reinforcing [[systems/feedback-loops|feedback loops]]. They explain winner-take-most markets, platform lock-in, and why standards battles are fought so fiercely — dynamics that adoption games in [[systems/game-theory|game theory]] make precise.

## Where they appear

- Communication systems: telephones, email, messaging apps
- Platforms and marketplaces that match two sides (buyers and sellers)
- Protocols and standards, where compatibility is the product
- Knowledge networks, where each new link raises the value of the graph
