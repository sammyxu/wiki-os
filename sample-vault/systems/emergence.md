# Emergence

Emergence is the appearance of properties and patterns at the level of a whole system that none of its individual components possess on their own.

The physicist Philip W. Anderson gave the idea its modern slogan in his 1972 essay "More Is Different," arguing that each level of organization obeys principles that cannot simply be deduced from the level below. Emergence is a central preoccupation of [[systems/complexity-theory|complexity theory]] and is closely tied to [[systems/self-organization|self-organization]]: local interactions among simple parts, repeated many times, generate global order that no part intended.

## Classic Examples

- Ant colonies allocate labor and build structures although no ant holds a blueprint.
- Craig Reynolds' 1986 "Boids" simulation showed lifelike flocking arising from just three local steering rules.
- Market prices aggregate dispersed knowledge that no single participant possesses.
- Consciousness is often described as an emergent property of neurons, a claim debated within the [[philosophy/philosophy-of-mind|philosophy of mind]].

Darwin's account of adaptation in the [[science/theory-of-evolution|theory of evolution]] is perhaps the deepest example: complex design emerging from variation and selection, with no designer required.
