# Cybernetics

Cybernetics is the transdisciplinary study of control and communication in machines, living organisms, and societies, built on the idea of circular causal feedback.

The field was named by [[people/norbert-wiener|Norbert Wiener]] in his 1948 book *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*, taking the term from the Greek *kybernetes*, "steersman." Its core insight — that goal-seeking behavior can be explained by [[systems/feedback-loops|feedback loops]] comparing actual state to desired state — grew out of Wiener's wartime work on anti-aircraft fire control. The Macy Conferences (1946–1953) brought Wiener together with John von Neumann, Warren McCulloch, and anthropologist Margaret Mead, making cybernetics a genuine meeting point of engineering, biology, and social science. W. Ross Ashby's *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956) added the law of requisite variety: a controller must be at least as varied as the disturbances it regulates.

## Legacy

Cybernetics seeded modern control engineering and early [[computing/artificial-intelligence|artificial intelligence]], and it developed alongside [[systems/information-theory|information theory]], created by Wiener's contemporary [[people/claude-shannon|Claude Shannon]]. Its vocabulary of feedback, homeostasis, and regulation is now foundational to [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]].
