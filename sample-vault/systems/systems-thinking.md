# Systems Thinking

Systems thinking is the practice of understanding phenomena not as isolated parts but as interconnected wholes, where structure drives behavior and relationships matter as much as components.

The field draws on the general systems theory that biologist Ludwig von Bertalanffy developed in the 1940s, which sought laws common to all organized wholes, and on the work of Donella Meadows, whose 1972 study *The Limits to Growth* and later book *Thinking in Systems* made stocks, flows, and leverage points part of the everyday analytical vocabulary. What unites these traditions is a shift of attention from things to the patterns of connection between things. The notes below map the major branches of the field.

## Feedback and Control

- [[systems/feedback-loops|Feedback Loops]] — the circular chains of cause and effect that stabilize or amplify a system's behavior.
- [[systems/cybernetics|Cybernetics]] — Norbert Wiener's science of control and communication in animals and machines.
- [[systems/self-organization|Self-Organization]] — how order arises spontaneously from local interactions, with no central planner.

## Complexity and Emergence

- [[systems/emergence|Emergence]] — wholes exhibiting properties that none of their parts possess.
- [[systems/complexity-theory|Complexity Theory]] — the study of complex adaptive systems, pioneered at the Santa Fe Institute.
- [[systems/chaos-theory|Chaos Theory]] — deterministic systems whose sensitivity to initial conditions makes them unpredictable in practice.

## Information, Strategy, and Scale

- [[systems/information-theory|Information Theory]] — Claude Shannon's mathematics of communication, uncertainty, and entropy.
- [[systems/game-theory|Game Theory]] — the formal analysis of strategic interaction among interdependent agents.
- [[systems/network-effects|Network Effects]] — why a system's value can grow faster than its size.
- [[systems/pareto-principle|Pareto Principle]] — the recurring 80/20 asymmetry between inputs and outcomes.
