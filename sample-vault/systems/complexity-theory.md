# Complexity Theory

Complexity theory studies systems made of many interacting agents whose collective behavior adapts, evolves, and cannot be reduced to simple laws governing the parts.

The field crystallized around the Santa Fe Institute, founded in 1984 by George Cowan with physicists including Nobel laureate Murray Gell-Mann. There, John Holland developed the idea of the complex adaptive system — ecosystems, immune systems, economies — whose agents learn and co-evolve, while Christopher Langton described computation and life as flourishing at the "edge of chaos," a regime poised between rigid order and randomness. Precursors include [[people/john-von-neumann|John von Neumann]]'s self-reproducing cellular automata of the 1940s.

## Key Ideas

- [[systems/emergence|Emergence]]: macro-level patterns that no micro-level rule mentions.
- [[systems/self-organization|Self-organization]]: order arising without central control.
- Nonlinearity: small causes can have large effects, the territory of [[systems/chaos-theory|chaos theory]].
- Adaptation: agents change their rules in response to the system they are part of.

Complexity science gives [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]] much of its mathematical and computational toolkit, from agent-based models to network analysis.
