# Feedback Loops

A feedback loop exists when a system's output is routed back as one of its inputs, so that past behavior shapes future behavior in a circle of cause and effect.

Feedback comes in two flavors. Reinforcing (positive) loops amplify change: compound interest, viral growth, and [[systems/network-effects|network effects]] all snowball because success feeds further success. Balancing (negative) loops resist change and seek equilibrium — a thermostat measures room temperature and adjusts heating to close the gap with its setpoint, the canonical example studied by [[systems/cybernetics|cybernetics]] pioneers such as [[people/norbert-wiener|Norbert Wiener]] in the 1940s.

Jay Forrester's system dynamics group, founded at MIT in 1956, turned feedback analysis into computer simulation, and Donella Meadows applied those models to global resource limits in *The Limits to Growth* (1972). Diagramming interlocking loops remains one of the core tools of [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]].

## Why Loops Matter

Delays inside a balancing loop cause oscillation, which is why a slow shower faucet produces alternating scalding and freezing water. Reinforcing loops left unchecked produce exponential growth or collapse, and networks of coupled loops can drive [[systems/self-organization|self-organization]], where stable large-scale order appears without anyone designing it.
