# Game Theory

Game theory is the formal study of strategic interaction — situations where the best choice for each participant depends on what everyone else chooses. It supplies a common language for economics, evolutionary biology, political science, and computer science.

The modern field begins with [[people/john-von-neumann|John von Neumann]], who proved the minimax theorem in 1928 and, with economist Oskar Morgenstern, published *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* in 1944. In 1950 John Nash extended the theory beyond zero-sum games with his equilibrium concept, for which he later shared the Nobel Prize. The same year, researchers at RAND formulated the prisoner's dilemma, still the canonical illustration of how individually rational choices can produce collectively poor outcomes.

Game theory sits comfortably inside [[systems/systems-thinking|systems thinking]]: strategies, payoffs, and repeated play form [[systems/feedback-loops|feedback loops]] that can lock systems into equilibria or tip them into new ones. Adoption games with increasing returns underlie [[systems/network-effects|network effects]], and evolutionary game theory shows cooperation emerging from selfish agents — a theme shared with [[systems/emergence|emergence]].

## Core concepts

- Nash equilibrium: no player gains by unilaterally changing strategy
- Zero-sum versus positive-sum games
- The prisoner's dilemma and the tragedy of the commons
- Repeated games, reputation, and the evolution of cooperation
