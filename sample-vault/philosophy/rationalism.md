# Rationalism

Rationalism is the view that reason, rather than sensory experience, is the chief source and final test of genuine knowledge.

Its modern founder is René Descartes, whose *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641) doubted everything the senses report and rebuilt knowledge from the indubitable *cogito, ergo sum*. Baruch Spinoza's *Ethics* (1677) deduced an entire metaphysics in geometric style, and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — co-inventor of [[science/calculus|calculus]] — argued that necessary truths can never be established by observation alone. The school's great rival was [[philosophy/empiricism|empiricism]], and the dispute between them ran until Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) proposed that experience and reason jointly structure knowledge. The debate is charted in [[philosophy/schools-of-thought|Schools of Thought]].

## Key Commitments

- Some ideas, such as those of mathematics, are innate rather than learned from experience.
- Deduction from [[philosophy/first-principles|first principles]] yields certainty that observation cannot.
- Reality has an intelligible structure that reason is fitted to grasp.
