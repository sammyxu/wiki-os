# Empiricism

Empiricism is the epistemological position that knowledge derives primarily from sensory experience rather than from innate ideas or pure reason.

The tradition took modern shape in Britain. Francis Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620) urged systematic observation over inherited doctrine; John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) argued the mind begins as a blank slate; and David Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) pushed the view to its skeptical limits, questioning whether experience can even justify belief in causation. Empiricism defined itself against [[philosophy/rationalism|rationalism]], the school of Descartes and Leibniz, until Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) attempted to reconcile the two. Both camps appear in the wider map of [[philosophy/schools-of-thought|Schools of Thought]].

## Legacy in Science

The empiricist insistence on observation and experiment became a cornerstone of the [[science/scientific-method|scientific method]]. [[people/isaac-newton|Isaac Newton]]'s optical experiments and Hume's problem of induction still frame how [[philosophy/epistemology|epistemology]] treats scientific evidence today.
