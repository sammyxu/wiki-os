# Occam's Razor

Occam's Razor is the principle of parsimony: when competing explanations account for the same evidence, prefer the one that makes the fewest assumptions. It is a rule of thumb for theory choice, not a guarantee that nature is simple.

The maxim is named for William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347), an English Franciscan friar and scholastic logician, though the canonical Latin phrasing — *entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*, "entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity" — was coined by later writers. The razor became a working tool of the [[science/scientific-method|scientific method]]: Isaac Newton enshrined it in the *Principia* as his first Rule of Reasoning, admitting no more causes than are "true and sufficient" to explain the phenomena.

## Why Simplicity Works

Simpler theories are easier to test and harder to rescue with ad hoc patches, a virtue prized by [[philosophy/empiricism]] and studied formally in modern statistics as the trade-off behind overfitting. Its justification remains a live question in [[philosophy/epistemology]] and across the empirical [[philosophy/schools-of-thought]]: why should elegance track truth at all?
