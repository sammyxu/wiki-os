# Ethics of Technology

Ethics of technology asks how the tools we build reshape human values, and what responsibilities inventors, engineers, and societies bear for the consequences. It treats artifacts not as neutral instruments but as choices with moral weight.

The field's modern founding text is [[people/norbert-wiener|Norbert Wiener]]'s *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950), which warned that automation would transform labor and demanded that machines serve human dignity. Hans Jonas's *The Imperative of Responsibility* (1979) extended ethics to future generations affected by powerful technologies, while Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980) argued that designs themselves can embed power relations. Many practical debates borrow the cost-benefit reasoning of [[philosophy/utilitarianism]], weighing aggregate harms against benefits, though critics across the [[philosophy/schools-of-thought|schools of thought]] object that rights and justice resist such arithmetic.

Today the sharpest questions surround [[computing/artificial-intelligence]]: algorithmic bias, surveillance, autonomous weapons, and the alignment of machine goals with human ones. The [[computing/open-source-movement|open source movement]] frames software freedom itself as an ethical stance, while platform [[systems/network-effects|network effects]] concentrate influence in ways no single designer intended.

## Recurring questions

- Who is accountable when an autonomous system causes harm?
- Should some technologies not be built at all?
- How do we weigh present convenience against long-term risk?
