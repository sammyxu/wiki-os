# Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is the ethical theory that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. It judges acts by their consequences rather than by rules, intentions, or character.

Jeremy Bentham laid out the doctrine in *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), even proposing a "felicific calculus" for weighing pleasures and pains. John Stuart Mill refined it in *Utilitarianism* (1863), arguing that pleasures differ in quality, not just quantity. Among the [[philosophy/schools-of-thought]], it stands as the leading form of consequentialism, often contrasted with duty-based ethics and with virtue traditions such as [[philosophy/stoicism]].

## Influence and Criticism

Later thinkers split the view into act and rule utilitarianism, and Peter Singer extended it to animal welfare and global poverty. Critics object that it can sanction injustice toward minorities if the aggregate benefit is large enough. The framework remains central to cost-benefit analysis, public policy, and debates in the [[philosophy/ethics-of-technology|ethics of technology]], where questions of aggregating welfare connect it to formal tools like [[systems/game-theory|game theory]].
