# Philosophy of Mind

Philosophy of mind examines the nature of consciousness, thought, and the relationship between mental states and the physical brain. Its central puzzle, how subjective experience arises from matter, remains one of the oldest open questions across the [[philosophy/schools-of-thought|schools of thought]].

The modern debate begins with René Descartes, whose *Meditations* (1641) argued for substance dualism: mind and body as fundamentally different kinds of thing, a view rooted in [[philosophy/rationalism]]. Gilbert Ryle attacked this picture in *The Concept of Mind* (1949), mocking the Cartesian "ghost in the machine," while later physicalists and functionalists such as Hilary Putnam argued that mental states are defined by what they do, not what they are made of.

The field intersects computing directly. [[people/alan-turing|Alan Turing]]'s 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed the imitation game as a practical test for machine thought, framing questions that still drive [[computing/artificial-intelligence]]. John Searle's Chinese Room argument (1980) and David Chalmers's "hard problem" of consciousness (1995) pushed back, asking whether symbol manipulation can ever amount to understanding.

## Key positions

- Dualism: mind and matter are distinct substances (Descartes)
- Physicalism: mental states are brain states
- Functionalism: mind is defined by causal roles, so it could run on silicon
- Emergentism: consciousness arises from complex organization, a case of [[systems/emergence]]
