# Moore's Law

Moore's Law is the observation that the number of components on an integrated circuit doubles at a regular interval, roughly every two years. For half a century it served as both a forecast and a self-imposed schedule for the semiconductor industry.

Gordon Moore, then research director at Fairchild Semiconductor, made the observation in a 1965 *Electronics* article, "Cramming More Components onto Integrated Circuits," predicting a doubling every year; in 1975 he revised the pace to every two years. Caltech's Carver Mead popularized the name "Moore's Law." The prediction held because the industry treated it as a roadmap: chipmakers planned factories, budgets, and product cycles around hitting the next doubling, a self-fulfilling [[systems/feedback-loops|feedback loop]] as much as a law of physics.

## Consequences

- Each shrink made every [[computing/transistor|transistor]] on an [[computing/integrated-circuit|integrated circuit]] faster, cheaper, and less power-hungry.
- Falling cost per computation enabled the [[computing/personal-computer|personal computer]], smartphones, and data centers.
- Since the mid-2010s, transistor scaling has slowed as features approach atomic dimensions, pushing designers toward specialized chips — a turning point in the [[computing/history-of-computing|history of computing]].
