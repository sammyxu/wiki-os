# ENIAC

ENIAC, the Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, was the first general-purpose electronic digital computer, completed at the University of Pennsylvania in 1945. Built from roughly 17,500 vacuum tubes, it computed artillery firing tables about a thousand times faster than any prior machine.

Designed by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert for the U.S. Army, ENIAC filled a 30-by-50-foot room and drew 150 kilowatts. It was programmed by physically rewiring plugboards and switches — laborious work carried out by six pioneering women, including Jean Bartik and Betty Holberton, whose contributions went largely unrecognized for decades. Consulting on the project, [[people/john-von-neumann|John von Neumann]] wrote the 1945 EDVAC report describing the [[computing/stored-program-concept|stored-program concept]], which replaced rewiring with instructions held in memory and defined the architecture of every computer since.

## Legacy

Eckert and Mauchly went on to build UNIVAC, the first commercial American computer, where [[people/grace-hopper|Grace Hopper]] developed the first compiler and championed programming in something closer to English. ENIAC's power-hungry vacuum tubes were soon displaced by the [[computing/transistor|transistor]], setting off the miniaturization that made computing ubiquitous.
