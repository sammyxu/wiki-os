# ARPANET

ARPANET was the first operational packet-switched network and the direct ancestor of the modern internet, funded by the U.S. Advanced Research Projects Agency. Its story is a turning point in the [[computing/history-of-computing|history of computing]].

The first message was sent on October 29, 1969, from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute, home of [[people/douglas-engelbart|Douglas Engelbart]]'s lab, which became the network's second node. The system crashed after the letters "LO" of "LOGIN", but within months four nodes were exchanging traffic. Email arrived in 1971, and on January 1, 1983, the network switched to the TCP/IP protocol suite, whose spread was accelerated by its inclusion in Berkeley [[computing/unix|Unix]]. ARPANET was decommissioned in 1990, just as [[computing/world-wide-web|the World Wide Web]] was being built on the internet it had bootstrapped.

## Why packet switching mattered

Instead of dedicating a circuit to each conversation, packet switching breaks messages into independently routed chunks, making the network resilient and efficient. Each new host made the network more valuable to every other host, an early demonstration of [[systems/network-effects|network effects]].
