# Personal Computer

The personal computer took computing out of the machine room and put it on the desks of individuals, turning a shared institutional resource into an everyday personal tool. It is one of the pivotal chapters in the [[computing/history-of-computing|history of computing]].

The 1975 Altair 8800 kit ignited the hobbyist era, followed by the Apple II in 1977 and the IBM PC in 1981, which made microcomputers a mass-market product. None of it would have been affordable without the [[computing/transistor|transistor]] and the [[computing/integrated-circuit|integrated circuit]], whose relentless cost-per-component decline was famously described by [[computing/moores-law|Moore's Law]]. Much of the interactive vision came earlier: [[people/douglas-engelbart|Douglas Engelbart]]'s 1968 "Mother of All Demos" showed the mouse, windows, and collaborative editing, ideas later refined at Xerox PARC on the Alto and popularized by the Macintosh in 1984.

## From hobbyists to households

- 1975: MITS Altair 8800 launches the microcomputer hobbyist boom
- 1977: Apple II, Commodore PET, and TRS-80 arrive as ready-to-use machines
- 1981: IBM PC sets an open hardware standard that clones rapidly adopt
- 1984: Apple Macintosh brings the graphical interface to the mainstream
