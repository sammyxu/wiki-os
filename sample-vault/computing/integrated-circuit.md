# Integrated Circuit

The integrated circuit places many transistors, resistors, and their interconnections on a single chip of semiconductor material. It solved the "tyranny of numbers" — the impossibility of hand-wiring millions of discrete components — and set computing on its exponential path.

Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments demonstrated the first working integrated circuit in September 1958, built on germanium with gold-wire connections. In 1959 Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor independently invented a silicon version using the planar process, in which interconnections are printed photographically — the approach all modern chips still follow. Kilby received the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics; Noyce went on to co-found Intel. Early customers included the Apollo Guidance Computer of the [[space/apollo-program|Apollo program]], whose demand helped drive prices down.

## The Exponential Engine

Because each [[computing/transistor|transistor]] on a chip shrank with every manufacturing generation, chips grew steadily denser and cheaper per component — a trend Gordon Moore quantified in 1965 as [[computing/moores-law|Moore's Law]]. That scaling turned the microprocessor into a commodity, enabled the [[computing/personal-computer|personal computer]], and underpins nearly every later chapter of the [[computing/history-of-computing|history of computing]].
