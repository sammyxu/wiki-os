# Enigma Codebreaking

Enigma codebreaking was the Allied effort during World War II to decrypt German military messages enciphered by the rotor-based Enigma machine. Centered at Bletchley Park in England, it became one of history's most consequential exercises in applied computation.

Building on prewar breakthroughs by Polish cryptologists Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki, and Henryk Zygalski, [[people/alan-turing|Alan Turing]] and Gordon Welchman designed the Bombe, an electromechanical device that searched thousands of rotor settings by exploiting predictable phrases in intercepted traffic. By 1943 the intelligence it produced, codenamed Ultra, was shaping the Battle of the Atlantic and Allied strategy more broadly; historians credit it with shortening the war by years. The same site later built Colossus to attack the Lorenz cipher, arguably the first programmable electronic computer, though its existence stayed secret for decades.

## From Codebreaking to Computing

Bletchley demonstrated that machines could automate reasoning at scale, a direct bridge from the abstract [[computing/turing-machine|Turing machine]] to working electronic hardware such as [[computing/eniac|ENIAC]]. The mathematics of secrecy it drew on was later formalized by [[people/claude-shannon|Claude Shannon]] within [[systems/information-theory|information theory]].
