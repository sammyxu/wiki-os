# Analytical Engine

The Analytical Engine was a general-purpose mechanical computer designed by [[people/charles-babbage|Charles Babbage]] beginning in 1837. Though never completed in his lifetime, it anticipated nearly every element of modern computing a century before electronics existed.

The design separated a "mill" for arithmetic from a "store" for holding numbers — a division of labor echoed in today's processors and memory — and took instructions from punched cards borrowed from the Jacquard loom. It supported conditional branching and loops, which is what elevated it from calculator to computer. In 1843, [[people/ada-lovelace|Ada Lovelace]] published an extensive set of notes on the machine, including a step-by-step method for computing Bernoulli numbers that is often called the first computer program. She also speculated that such an engine might one day manipulate symbols and music, not just numbers — a glimpse of ideas later formalized in the [[computing/turing-machine|Turing Machine]] and pursued by [[computing/artificial-intelligence|artificial intelligence]].

## Why It Failed to Be Built

Victorian machine tools could not economically produce the thousands of precision brass parts required, and Babbage's frequent redesigns exhausted both government funding and goodwill. The [[computing/stored-program-concept|stored-program concept]] would eventually realize his architecture in electronic form.
