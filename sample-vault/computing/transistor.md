# Transistor

The transistor is a semiconductor device that amplifies and switches electrical signals. By replacing the hot, fragile vacuum tube, it made computers small, cheap, and reliable enough to leave the laboratory.

The first working transistor was demonstrated at Bell Labs in December 1947 by John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, working under William Shockley, who soon devised the more practical junction transistor; the three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. The contrast with tube machines was stark: [[computing/eniac|ENIAC]] used some 17,000 vacuum tubes, drew about 150 kilowatts, and suffered regular tube failures. Silicon transistors, commercialized by Texas Instruments in 1954, proved rugged and mass-producible, and by the late 1950s transistorized computers had displaced tube designs.

## From Component to Chip

The transistor's real legacy was miniaturization. Packing many transistors onto a single sliver of silicon produced the [[computing/integrated-circuit|integrated circuit]], whose exponential improvement was later codified as [[computing/moores-law|Moore's Law]]. That trajectory carried computing from room-sized mainframes to the [[computing/personal-computer|personal computer]] and beyond, making the transistor arguably the pivotal invention in the [[computing/history-of-computing|history of computing]].
