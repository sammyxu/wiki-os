# Turing Machine

The Turing machine is a mathematical model of computation introduced by [[people/alan-turing|Alan Turing]] in his 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers." With nothing but an infinite tape, a read-write head, and a table of rules, it captures everything any digital computer can in principle do.

Turing devised the machine to settle David Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem, proving that no general procedure can decide the truth of every mathematical statement. Along the way he described a universal machine — one that reads the description of any other machine from its tape and simulates it. That single idea, a machine whose behavior is defined by data, foreshadowed the [[computing/stored-program-concept|stored-program concept]] and underlies all modern software. The Church-Turing thesis, formulated alongside Alonzo Church's work, holds that this model captures the intuitive notion of effective computation itself.

## Legacy

The model gives computer science its yardsticks: complexity classes, decidability, and the halting problem all live on Turing's tape. It connects to [[systems/information-theory|information theory]] through the shared question of what symbols can encode, informed Turing's practical work on [[computing/enigma-codebreaking|Enigma codebreaking]], and framed his later question — "Can machines think?" — that launched [[computing/artificial-intelligence|artificial intelligence]].
