# Digital Gardens

A digital garden is a personal website of interlinked notes that grow over time, published while still imperfect. Unlike a blog's reverse-chronological stream, a garden is organized by topic and tended continuously.

The metaphor traces to Mark Bernstein's 1998 essay "Hypertext Gardens," and Mike Caulfield's 2015 talk "The Garden and the Stream" sharpened the contrast with feed-based media. The form is a direct descendant of [[computing/hypertext]] — Ted Nelson's vision of documents connected by links rather than locked in sequence — brought to everyday practice on the [[computing/world-wide-web]] by tools like TiddlyWiki, Obsidian, and Quartz. Writers such as Andy Matuschak and Maggie Appleton pushed the idea further, publishing [[writing/evergreen-notes|evergreen notes]] that are revised for years instead of posted once and abandoned.

## Gardening principles

- Notes are living documents: revisit, revise, and re-link them as your understanding changes.
- Dense internal links matter more than polish; the graph is the garden's paths.
- Learning in public invites correction, making the garden part of a larger practice of [[writing/knowledge-work]].
