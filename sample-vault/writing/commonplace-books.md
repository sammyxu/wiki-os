# Commonplace Books

A commonplace book is a personal compilation of quotations, arguments, observations, and reflections, gathered from reading and daily life and organized for later reuse. For centuries it was the standard technology of [[writing/knowledge-work]].

The practice runs deep in Western letters. The *Meditations* of [[people/marcus-aurelius]] began as a private notebook of maxims in the tradition of [[philosophy/stoicism]]. Renaissance humanists such as Erasmus taught students to harvest passages under thematic headings, and in 1706 John Locke published *A New Method of Making Common-Place-Books*, describing an indexing scheme that let a reader retrieve any entry quickly. Milton, Jefferson, and Darwin all kept commonplace books, mining them for sermons, statutes, and science alike.

## From notebook to network

Where the commonplace book files ideas under fixed headings, the [[writing/zettelkasten]] links them laterally, and modern [[writing/digital-gardens]] publish the whole collection as a browsable web. The instinct is unchanged: capture what strikes you while reading — a habit worth pairing with a living [[notes/reading-list]] — and return to it until it becomes your own.
