# Atomic Notes

An atomic note captures exactly one idea, stated completely enough to stand on its own. Atomicity is the structural rule that makes a linked note collection composable rather than a pile of documents.

The principle comes from Niklas Luhmann's slip-box practice, where each card held a single claim so it could be referenced from many contexts — the foundation of the [[writing/zettelkasten]]. When a note contains one idea, a link to it is precise; when it contains five, every link is ambiguous. Sönke Ahrens's 2017 book *How to Take Smart Notes* popularized the rule for digital tools, and Andy Matuschak sharpened it with the maxim that notes should be "concept-oriented": titled after the idea itself, not the source it came from.

## Small pieces, loosely joined

- One idea per note keeps links unambiguous and reuse cheap.
- Declarative titles ("Spacing improves retention") let a note be read from its link alone.
- Atomic claims are easy to test and review, which is why they pair well with [[writing/spaced-repetition]].
- Atomicity is the precondition for [[writing/evergreen-notes]]: you can only revise an idea over years if it lives in one findable place.
